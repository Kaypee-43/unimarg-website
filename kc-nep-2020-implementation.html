NEP 2020: what it means for your institution, and how to align.

The National Education Policy 2020 is often discussed as a vision document. For a university or college it is something more concrete: a redesign of how programmes are built, how credits are counted, and how students move through them. The policy itself is settled; what keeps changing is the stream of UGC frameworks and guidelines that turn it into rules an institution has to follow. This guide sets out what has actually changed for higher education, and a practical sequence for aligning to it.

By UniMarg Higher Education Consultancy Reviewed by the UniMarg advisory team, October 2026 11 min read

The short answer

NEP 2020 asks institutions to become multidisciplinary, flexible and credit-based. In day-to-day terms that comes down to a handful of concrete changes: a four-year undergraduate programme with the option to exit early and return later, credits stored in a national digital account and recognised across institutions, wider subject choice, and newer provisions such as two degrees at once and admissions twice a year.

None of this is a single filing. It is a set of academic and administrative decisions that touch curriculum, examinations, the academic calendar, student records and faculty workload at the same time. Institutions that treat NEP as a circular to be acknowledged tend to implement it on paper and struggle when a NAAC or NBA team looks for it in practice.

NEP alignment is not a compliance note. It is a redesign of the programme, the credit system and the policies around them.

Handled well, the same work also strengthens your next accreditation cycle.

The shift in one sentence

The old model was a fixed three-year degree, a defined set of subjects, and one way through. The NEP model is a programme a student can enter and leave at defined points, built from credits that are portable, with real choice across disciplines. Everything below is a consequence of that shift.

The four-year undergraduate programme and multiple entry and exit

This is the change most visible to students, and the one that reshapes the most inside an institution. Under the UGC Curriculum and Credit Framework for Undergraduate Programmes, a degree can be taken as three years or four, with defined earlier exit points.

Two operational points matter more than the numbers. First, a student who exits can re-enter within three years and must complete the programme within a maximum of seven years, so your records and re-admission process have to hold a student's position open across a gap. Second, the Honours with Research track is open to students who reach a defined academic threshold, commonly reported as 75 percent or equivalent across the first three years, which means the final year is not identical for every student.

Designing for this is harder than running a single fixed cohort. The curriculum has to make sense at each exit, not only at the end, and examinations, transcripts and the academic calendar all have to carry the structure.

The Academic Bank of Credits and the National Credit Framework

Multiple entry and exit only works if credits are portable, and that is what two national structures are for.

The Academic Bank of Credits is a digital repository that stores the credits each student earns and allows them to be transferred between institutions. For an aligning institution, the practical requirement is that programmes are registered, students are enrolled on the platform, and credits are deposited reliably, because a credit that is not banked cannot be carried forward or recognised elsewhere.

The National Credit Framework is the structure that assigns credits across school, higher and vocational education, including experiential and work-based learning. It was launched in 2023 and operationalised through a standard operating procedure in 2024. Mapping programmes to it is what lets academic, vocational and experiential learning carry comparable credit, and it sits alongside the National Higher Education Qualifications Framework, which defines the levels from first-year undergraduate through to doctoral study.

A further provision worth planning around: a defined share of credits, up to 40 percent in a given programme, may be earned through approved SWAYAM online courses, which can widen a student's choices without the institution building every course itself.

Two degrees at once, and admissions twice a year

Two newer flexibilities often prompt questions from boards.

A student may pursue two academic programmes at the same time, in one of three permitted combinations: two full-time programmes where the class timings do not overlap, one full-time programme with one in open, distance or online mode, or two programmes both in open, distance or online mode. The institution has to be able to administer, examine and certify both without a timetable clash.

Separately, the UGC has permitted institutions to run admissions in two cycles a year, around July to August and January to February, rather than a single annual intake. This is an option, not an obligation. It can help fill seats and support re-entry, but it roughly doubles the admissions, orientation and examination cycle, so it is a decision to cost properly rather than adopt by default.

Multidisciplinary provision, and the direction of travel

NEP 2020 sets a direction more than a single deadline, and it is worth separating the firm requirements above from the longer-term policy aims.

Government material states the intent for all higher education institutions to become multidisciplinary by 2040, with at least one large multidisciplinary institution in or near every district by 2030, and a national gross enrolment ratio of 50 percent by 2035. The affiliation system is intended to be replaced gradually by graded autonomy based on performance, rather than abolished on a fixed date.

A single regulator, the Higher Education Commission of India, is proposed to replace the current set of regulators, but it has not been established. Until it is, the UGC, AICTE and the statutory councils continue to regulate as they do now. The sensible planning assumption is the current framework, with an eye on the direction, not a bet on a structure that does not yet exist.

Separate what is a rule today from what is a stated aim for 2035 or 2040. Build to the rules now, and plan the aims into your five-year roadmap.

Why this sits with accreditation, not apart from it

NAAC, NBA and NIRF frameworks increasingly look for exactly what NEP asks institutions to build: curriculum flexibility, a credit-based structure, multidisciplinary provision, and functioning multiple entry and exit. The curriculum and credit decisions you take for NEP are the same ones an assessment team will examine, so handling the two together saves duplicated effort and avoids the common trap of an institution that is NEP-compliant on paper but cannot show it working on the day.

Our guidance on recognition, accreditation and regulatory approvals treats NEP alignment and accreditation readiness as one workstream for this reason.

A practical sequence for aligning

Alignment is manageable when it is sequenced rather than attempted all at once.

  • Gap study. Assess current programmes, credit structures and policies against the UGC frameworks, and identify what genuinely needs to change versus what already complies.
  • Curriculum and credit redesign. Rebuild programmes around the credit framework and the exit points, so each stage is coherent on its own and multidisciplinary choice is real rather than nominal.
  • Systems. Register on the Academic Bank of Credits, map programmes to the National Credit Framework, and make sure the student information system can hold credits, exits and re-entries.
  • Policies and the calendar. Update the examination, evaluation, admission and academic-calendar policies, including any move to two intakes a year.
  • Faculty and operations readiness. Prepare faculty and administrative teams for choice-based teaching, credit accounting and the heavier operational load, because this is where paper alignment usually breaks.

Where institutions go wrong

  • Treating NEP as a notification. Acknowledging the policy without redesigning the programme leaves the institution exposed when alignment is actually inspected.
  • Banking credits late. If credits are not deposited in the Academic Bank of Credits reliably, multiple entry and exit does not function for the student it is meant to help.
  • Designing only the end. A four-year curriculum that does not make sense at the one and two-year exits fails the students who leave at those points.
  • Adopting two intakes without costing them. A second annual cycle doubles a good deal of operational work and should be a deliberate choice.
  • Betting on structures that do not exist yet. Planning around a regulator or a deadline that has not arrived, rather than the current framework, wastes effort.
  • Reading dated guidance. Much of what is published online predates the current UGC frameworks and guidelines.

Questions

Frequently asked questions.

What does NEP 2020 change for higher education institutions?

It moves institutions towards multidisciplinary, flexible, credit-based education. In practice that means a four-year undergraduate programme with multiple entry and exit, credits held in the Academic Bank of Credits and mapped to the National Credit Framework, broader subject choice, and provisions such as dual degrees and twice-yearly admissions. For a university or college it is a redesign of curriculum, credit structures and academic policy, not a single filing.

What is the four-year undergraduate programme under NEP 2020?

Under the UGC Curriculum and Credit Framework for Undergraduate Programmes, a bachelor's degree can be three years and 120 credits, or a four-year Honours degree of 160 credits, with a four-year Honours with Research track for students who meet an academic threshold in the first three years. Students may exit earlier with a certificate after one year or a diploma after two years and re-enter later.

What are the credit requirements and exit points in the four-year programme?

A certificate is awarded after one year at 40 credits plus a four-credit summer vocational course, a diploma after two years at 80 credits plus a four-credit summer vocational course, a bachelor's degree after three years at 120 credits, and a four-year Honours degree at 160 credits. A student who exits may re-enter within three years and must complete the programme within a maximum of seven years.

What is the Academic Bank of Credits?

The Academic Bank of Credits is a national digital repository that stores the credits a student earns and allows them to be transferred between institutions. It is what makes multiple entry and exit and credit mobility work in practice, so an institution aligning with NEP 2020 needs its programmes registered and its students' credits deposited there.

What is the National Credit Framework (NCrF)?

The National Credit Framework is a single structure that assigns credits across school, higher and vocational education, including experiential and work-based learning. It was launched in 2023 and operationalised through a standard operating procedure in 2024. Institutions map their programmes to it so that academic, vocational and experiential learning carry comparable credit.

Can a student pursue two degrees at the same time under NEP 2020?

Yes. UGC guidelines allow a student to pursue two academic programmes simultaneously in one of three ways: two full-time programmes where the class timings do not overlap, one full-time and one in open or distance or online mode, or two programmes both in open or distance or online mode.

Are universities now admitting students twice a year?

The UGC has permitted institutions to admit students in two cycles a year, around July to August and January to February, instead of a single annual intake. It is an option an institution may adopt, not a requirement, and it has implications for the academic calendar, examinations and admissions operations.

How does NEP 2020 connect to NAAC and other accreditation?

Accreditation and ranking frameworks increasingly assess curriculum flexibility, credit-based structures, multidisciplinary provision and multiple entry and exit. NEP alignment and accreditation readiness are therefore best handled together rather than as separate projects, since the same curriculum and credit decisions feed both.

By when must institutions become multidisciplinary under NEP 2020?

The policy sets a direction rather than a single switch. Government material states the aim for all higher education institutions to become multidisciplinary by 2040, with at least one large multidisciplinary institution in or near every district by 2030, and a gross enrolment ratio of 50 percent by 2035. The affiliation system is intended to be gradually replaced by graded autonomy.

Has the Higher Education Commission of India replaced the UGC?

Not yet. A single regulator, the Higher Education Commission of India, is proposed under NEP 2020 to replace the current multiple regulators, but it has not been established. Until it is, the UGC, AICTE and the statutory councils continue to regulate as before, and institutions should plan against the current framework.

Knowledge Centre · Accreditation

The NAAC SSR and accreditation, as it works now.

The Self-Study Report is the heart of NAAC accreditation, and the part institutions most often underestimate. It is not a form filled in near the deadline; it is a year-round discipline of evidence that a peer team then examines. This guide explains what the SSR is, how the accreditation process runs around it, and what the current move to binary accreditation and maturity-based levels changes. Because the framework is being revised, it also marks clearly where the detail is still being settled.

By UniMarg Higher Education Consultancy Reviewed by the UniMarg advisory team, October 2026 11 min read

The short answer

NAAC accreditation comes down to a single document and what sits behind it. The Self-Study Report, the SSR, is the institution's own account of its quality across NAAC's criteria, part data and part narrative. Everything else in the process, the eligibility application, the data validation, the peer team visit, is a test of whether the SSR holds up.

The framework around that document is currently being reformed. NAAC is moving from the older letter-grade system to binary accreditation, Accredited or Not Accredited, followed by graded levels of maturity. The reform is still being rolled out, so some specifics are not yet fixed. What has not changed is the thing that actually decides outcomes: whether an institution has run a genuine quality system through the year, with documented evidence, rather than assembling a case at the last minute.

The SSR is not written at the end. It is the readout of a quality system that has been running all year.

Institutions that understand this prepare differently, and fare differently.

What the SSR actually is

The Self-Study Report is a structured self-assessment. It combines quantitative data, the measurable facts of the institution, with a qualitative narrative that explains practice and context. It is organised around NAAC's assessment criteria, and it is supported by evidence that must stand up to independent checking.

The report is prepared and owned by the institution's Internal Quality Assurance Cell. An IQAC that exists only on paper is the most common reason an SSR is weak, because the report is only as good as the quality processes it describes. Where the IQAC genuinely runs quality through the year, the SSR largely writes itself from records that already exist.

The seven criteria

NAAC has long assessed institutions across seven criteria, and the SSR is built around them. The metrics underneath each are being revised as part of the current reform, but the criteria themselves are the stable frame to prepare against.

CriterionWhat it covers
1. Curricular AspectsCurriculum design, flexibility, and how the institution keeps its programmes current and relevant.
2. Teaching, Learning and EvaluationAdmissions, teaching quality, the student-teacher ratio, and a fair, timely evaluation system.
3. Research, Innovations and ExtensionResearch output, funding, publications, collaborations and community engagement.
4. Infrastructure and Learning ResourcesPhysical and academic infrastructure, the library, IT, and their upkeep and use.
5. Student Support and ProgressionScholarships, guidance, placements, progression to higher study, and student activities.
6. Governance, Leadership and ManagementVision, strategy, participative governance, faculty welfare and financial management.
7. Institutional Values and Best PracticesInclusion, environmental consciousness, ethics, and the institution's distinctive practices.

How the process runs

The accreditation process is a sequence of gates, each examining the one before. The exact mechanics are being revised under the new framework, so treat the sequence below as the shape of the process rather than a fixed checklist, and confirm the current steps against NAAC's live portal.

  • A functioning IQAC. The quality cell that runs and documents quality through the year. This is the foundation, not a step.
  • IIQA. The Institutional Information for Quality Assessment, the eligibility application that must be accepted before the SSR can be submitted.
  • The SSR. The Self-Study Report itself, with its quantitative data and qualitative narrative, submitted with supporting documents.
  • Data validation. The Data Validation and Verification stage, where the quantitative claims are checked against evidence and corrected where they cannot be substantiated.
  • Student Satisfaction Survey. Direct feedback gathered from students, where applicable to the institution and cycle.
  • Peer team assessment. An external committee examines the institution against its SSR, historically through a physical visit and more recently in online or hybrid modes for some categories.
  • Result. NAAC declares the outcome. Under the new framework that outcome is a binary decision, with graded levels to follow.

What the reform changes

On the recommendations of the Dr K Radhakrishnan Committee, NAAC has been moving to a two-part model, introduced from 2025.

Binary accreditation

The first part is binary accreditation: an outcome of Accredited or Not Accredited against a set of minimum benchmarks, in place of the earlier letter grades. The intention is a clearer entry standard that more institutions can be assessed against, rather than a single number that compressed very different institutions into one grade.

Maturity-Based Graded Levels

The second part is Maturity-Based Graded Levels, or MBGL. Accredited institutions are to be placed on graded levels of maturity, commonly described as Levels 1 to 5 with a higher level for institutions of national and global excellence. This is where an institution demonstrates progression beyond the entry standard.

An honest caveat: the detailed benchmarks, metric counts and level definitions are being finalised by NAAC and should be treated as indicative until the official manuals are published. Anyone quoting precise thresholds today is quoting a moving target.

The transition

Institutions that already hold a grade under the earlier Revised Accreditation Framework have generally been allowed to retain its validity until the new systems are fully in place, and to choose their route once they are. Where your institution sits in this transition depends on your current grade and cycle, which is exactly the sort of detail to confirm against NAAC's current rules before committing to a path.

Prepare for what every version of the framework rewards: a real quality system, clean documented evidence, and honest self-assessment. The route can be decided once those are in place.

Where institutions go wrong

  • Treating the SSR as a writing task. It is an evidence task. A well-written report with thin evidence fails at data validation.
  • A paper IQAC. If the quality cell does not actually run quality through the year, there is nothing genuine for the SSR to report.
  • Assembling evidence at the end. Documentation gathered in a rush is incomplete and inconsistent, and it shows.
  • Over-claiming in the data. Numbers that cannot be substantiated are corrected downward at validation, which is worse than a modest honest figure.
  • Quoting old thresholds. A great deal of published guidance still describes the letter-grade system and metrics that are being replaced.
  • Waiting passively for clarity. The fundamentals can be built now; only the final route depends on the manuals.

Questions

Frequently asked questions.

What is the SSR in NAAC accreditation?

The Self-Study Report is the institution's own evidence-based account of its quality, organised around NAAC's assessment criteria. It combines quantitative data with a qualitative narrative and is the central document of the accreditation process. Almost everything that follows, data validation and the peer team visit, is an examination of what the SSR claims.

What are the steps in the NAAC accreditation process?

In broad terms: establish and run an Internal Quality Assurance Cell, file the eligibility application known as the IIQA, prepare and submit the Self-Study Report with its supporting data, go through data validation and verification, the Student Satisfaction Survey where applicable, and a peer team assessment, after which NAAC declares the result. The exact sequence is being revised as NAAC moves to its new framework, so confirm the current steps against NAAC's live portal and manual.

What are the seven NAAC criteria?

NAAC has long assessed institutions across seven criteria: Curricular Aspects; Teaching, Learning and Evaluation; Research, Innovations and Extension; Infrastructure and Learning Resources; Student Support and Progression; Governance, Leadership and Management; and Institutional Values and Best Practices. The SSR is organised around these criteria, and the metrics underneath them are being revised as part of the current reform.

What is binary accreditation in NAAC?

Binary accreditation, introduced from 2025 on the recommendations of the Dr K Radhakrishnan Committee, replaces the earlier letter-grade system with a straightforward outcome of Accredited or Not Accredited against a set of minimum benchmarks. It is intended as the entry stage of accreditation, after which institutions can progress to graded levels.

What are Maturity-Based Graded Levels?

Maturity-Based Graded Levels, or MBGL, are the second stage after binary accreditation, placing accredited institutions on graded levels of maturity, commonly described as Levels 1 to 5 with a higher level for an institution of national and global excellence. The detailed benchmarks for each level are being finalised by NAAC, so they should be treated as indicative until the manuals are published.

What happened to the A++ to C grades and CGPA?

The letter-grade system, in which institutions received grades such as A++ down to C based on a cumulative grade point average, is being replaced by binary accreditation and the maturity-based levels. During the transition, institutions already holding a grade under the earlier Revised Accreditation Framework have been allowed to retain its validity until the new systems are fully in place. Confirm your institution's position against NAAC's current transition rules.

What is the IIQA?

The Institutional Information for Quality Assessment is the eligibility application an institution files before it can submit a Self-Study Report. It confirms that the institution meets the basic conditions to be assessed, and a successful IIQA is the gate to the rest of the process.

What is the DVV process?

Data Validation and Verification is the stage where the quantitative data an institution submits is checked against supporting evidence. Claims that cannot be substantiated are revised, so the discipline of keeping documented evidence through the year, rather than assembling it at the end, is what decides how an institution fares at this stage.

How long does NAAC accreditation take to prepare for?

It depends on the institution's starting point, but meaningful preparation is best measured in quarters, not weeks. A functioning Internal Quality Assurance Cell, documented evidence gathered through the year, and an honest gap study against the criteria are what shorten the timeline. An institution that begins assembling evidence only when the cycle opens is usually already behind.

Should an institution wait for the new framework or apply now?

That is an institution-specific decision and depends on your current grade, your cycle and your readiness. The reform is still being rolled out, so the sensible approach is to prepare the fundamentals that matter under any version of the framework, a working quality cell, clean documented evidence and honest self-assessment, and decide the route once your position against the current rules is clear. We help institutions make that call.
